**Molecules Before Life: Physical Chemistry in Prebiotic Worlds**

How did chemistry become biology? Students often encounter the origin of life as a story about what may have happened on the early Earth. This summer school treats it instead as a scientific problem that can be tested, measured, and argued from evidence.

Before cells existed, simple molecules in water had to survive long enough to react, combine, and organise. They may have faced warm or hot conditions, changing pH, mineral surfaces, and hydrothermal environments where temperature and pressure varied sharply. Water could have supported chemical complexity — but it could also have destroyed fragile molecules. The central question of this programme is therefore simple but difficult: under what conditions could prebiotic molecules persist long enough to matter?

In 2027, the Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences at The University of Hong Kong will run an off-school laboratory programme for around 32 senior secondary students from the Hong Kong Academy for Gifted Education. Students will investigate early-Earth chemistry through hands-on laboratory work, using safe teaching-laboratory methods to explore the effects of temperature, pH, minerals, and spectroscopy on simple organic molecules.

Rather than rehearsing a fixed narrative about the origin of life, students will learn to think like experimental scientists. They will work in small teams, design and carry out an investigation, analyse their results, and present what their data show — including where the data are uncertain, messy, or difficult to interpret.

English will be the medium of instruction. Junior-secondary science is sufficient background; HKDSE Chemistry is not required.

### Programme Approach

The summer school will begin with an introduction to the physical and chemical conditions that may have existed on the early Earth. These include liquid water, warm or hot environments, possible acidity in early oceans, and hydrothermal systems with strong gradients in temperature, pressure, and chemistry.

Students will then examine how simple organic molecules behave under these conditions. These may include sugars, nucleic-acid components, and short fragments related to RNA and DNA. Through guided laboratory activities, students will investigate how such molecules persist, degrade, or change when exposed to heat, different pH conditions, and mineral surfaces.

As the programme progresses, students will move from structured experiments to mentored mini-projects. In small groups, they will develop a testable question, collect data, evaluate uncertainty, and prepare a final poster or short oral presentation. They will also write a short reflection on what they learned about scientific evidence, experimental limits, and the difficulty of reconstructing chemistry from the deep past.

### Learning Goals

By the end of the programme, students should be able to:

- explain why water, energy, temperature, pH, and mineral surfaces are likely to have shaped prebiotic chemistry;
- distinguish between a popular “origin of life” story and a scientific argument based on chemical stability, reaction rates, and evidence;
- understand basic ideas in chemical kinetics, including reaction rates and half-life;
- use safe laboratory techniques appropriate for a teaching laboratory;
- interpret simple experimental data, including uncertain or inconsistent results;
- describe how spectroscopy and other observational methods can be used to study molecules;
- present a team investigation clearly and honestly;
- report uncertainty, failed runs, and ambiguous results with academic integrity.

The aim is not for students to “solve” the origin of life. The aim is for them to experience how scientists ask practical questions about it: what can survive, what breaks down, what reacts, and how do we know?

### Scientific Context

The programme is based on research into the physical chemistry of prebiotic environments. Long before biology existed, simple molecules on the early Earth would have been shaped by environmental extremes: heat, acidity or alkalinity, pressure, dissolved minerals, and reactive surfaces. These factors may have determined whether organic molecules survived, decomposed, or became available for further chemistry.

By studying these processes in the laboratory, students will explore one of the central challenges in origin-of-life research: not merely how complex molecules could form, but how they could remain stable long enough to participate in further chemical evolution.
