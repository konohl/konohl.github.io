<b>:loudspeaker: Clusters, Nanodroplets, and Fluid Cavities in Hydrothermal Water, July. 10, 2025 </b> (Goldschmidt 2025 Prague, Session 05i Convener) :droplet: :microscope: :hole: :thermometer:

<div style="text-align: justify;">
<p>
Session 05i on Fluid-Mediated Subsurface Processes was a great success at Goldschmidt 2025 in Prague, featuring a excellent keynote talk by Joël Brugger on field and experimental insights into gold transport, speciation, deposition, and concentration in the Earth’s crust. The session included a diverse set of experimental, theoretical, and simulation-based talks exploring fluid-rock interactions and subsurface resource formation. The session also featured quite a few interesting MD and experimental talks focusing on water and solute behavior in the vicinity of the critical point.
</p>
<p>
<b>🗣 GS2025 talk ID31523 (Lemke & Hui):</b> Our molecular dynamics study presented at Goldschmidt 2025 built on some early pioneering simulations demonstrating spontaneous cavity formation in superheated water by Zahn (Phys.Rev.Lett. 2004, 93, 227801). Building on this work, we systematically probed the molecular structure and reactivity of water in the vicinity of the boiling point to determine how hydrothermal fluids prepare for phase separation - a process critical to metal mobility in ore-forming systems. MD simulations along the saturation curve revealed that rising temperature and density reduction promote nanoscale vacuum cavities in the liquid phase and progressive clustering in the vapor, with largest aggregates growing from ~(H₂O)₅ at 100°C to ~(H₂O)₄₀₀ size nanodroplets at 350°C accompanied by expanding pore space that weakens hydrogen bonding and initiates bubble nucleation. These confined environments significantly alter water ionization relative to the bulk, yielding a distinct pH and acidity with broad implications for geochemistry, geothermal energy, and high-temperature industrial processes such as nuclear reactor cooling. 


</p>
<p>
The graphic below shows snapshots from MD simulations of 1000 water molecules at increasing temperatures near the boiling point: (a) 100°C, (b) 200°C, (c) 300°C, and (d) 350°C. The green surfaces highlight empty void spaces within the water, which grow larger as the temperature rises and water becomes less dense—from about 45% voids at 100°C to 65% at 350°C. This feature showcases how water near its critical point develops an increasingly porous, "sponge-like" structure as interconnected cavities expand, thus paving the way for boiling and the transition to vapor. 📝 <b>Lemke, (2026)</b></p>
